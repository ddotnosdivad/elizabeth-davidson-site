If you've ever driven past Tualatin Commons and wondered about the tall steel sculpture at the corner, you've met "Lazy River." It went up in August 2014 to celebrate Tualatin's centennial, and it's packed with little stories about the city once you get close enough to look. It's also a great starting point for exploring the rest of Tualatin's public art, and the city says there are more than 250 pieces of it.

![The lake at Tualatin Commons on a sunny day, with shops and trees reflected in the still water](/assets/img/blog/tualatin-lazy-river-public-art.webp)

## Quick facts

- **What:** "Lazy River," a steel sculpture by artist Joseph Rastovich
- **Where:** Tualatin Commons Park, at the intersection of SW Martinazzi Ave, SW Nyberg St and SW Boones Ferry Rd
- **Installed:** August 2014, to commemorate Tualatin's centennial
- **Height:** 20 feet
- **Gift of:** the Tualatin Arts Advisory Committee
- **Citywide:** over 250 pieces of public art, according to the City of Tualatin

## The story behind Lazy River

According to the city's description of the piece, the sculpture's shape represents two things at once: the Tualatin River and a mastodon tusk. If the mastodon connection rings a bell, I wrote about [the Tualatin mastodon at the library](/post/tualatin-mastodon).

The surface is covered with 32 embossed icons of Tualatin's cultural and natural history. The city lists pioneers, crawfish, local fauna, the Ice Age floods, and biking and the river among them. My tip: walk all the way around it slowly. The icons are the kind of detail you miss from a car window.

The sculpture was a gift to the community from the Tualatin Arts Advisory Committee. The city says the committee gave it to strengthen community image and create a sense of place.

## Who keeps the art coming

The Tualatin Arts Advisory Committee was established in 1997, and the city says it has contributed over 3,000 hours of volunteer service to recognizing the arts in Tualatin. Its mission is to support and inspire the creation and integration of all art forms into the city's cultural heritage and future.

The committee has seven members serving three-year terms, plus one youth member serving a one-year term and a City Council liaison. It meets the third Tuesday of every month from 6:30 to 8 p.m., and the meetings are open to the public. It also offers small grants to promote the arts through the Arts Agency Contribution Program.

## Art on the signal boxes

![Traffic signal box in Tualatin wrapped in art of a humpback whale swimming through a starry galaxy](/assets/img/blog/tualatin-lazy-river-public-art-2.webp)

Some of my favorite Tualatin art is hiding in plain sight on traffic signal boxes, wrapped in vinyl artwork that covers all four sides and the top of each box. I'll share more about them in a future post, but if you're an artist, there's a call open right now. Applications opened September 30, 2026, and the deadline is **December 2, 2026**. The committee reviews entries December 17, public voting runs February 1-14, 2027, artists hear back February 21, and installation is planned for March through June 2027. Selected artists receive a $500 honorarium per box, and designs may not be generated by AI.

## The Tualatin Visual Chronicle

The Tualatin Visual Chronicle is a collection of works meant to show how the city has evolved and changed through the years, through the eyes of the artist. It serves as an archival record, and the works are displayed in public buildings throughout the city. That's a separate count from the city's public-art total: a 2023 Tualatin Historical Society article said the Arts Advisory Committee had purchased over 300 paintings, sculptures, photographs and other media over more than 25 years, and that a Student Visual Chronicle of youth work is part of the collection.

## The Living Room Gallery at the library

Inside the Tualatin Public Library, the Living Room Gallery is a rotating exhibit space around the central fireplace. This fall's featured artist is Kim McLaughlin, whose show "The Usual Suspects" focuses on film-noir imagery in oil on tinted paper. It's on display through mid-January 2027, so there's still time to see it.

## Seeing more of it

The city's online Parks and Public Art Map can be filtered to show only public art locations, which makes it easy to find pieces near you. A 2016 city brochure also describes an ArtWalk of signed loops starting with the 0.33-mile Commons Loop; I couldn't confirm the route signs are all still in place, so I'd lean on the map.

The Arts Advisory Committee also recognizes significant art endeavors through the Community Arts Enhancement Award. Past recipients include Don Armstrong's Chinook Salmon water feature (2011), the "I Wonder" bronze mastodon at Nyberg Rivers (2015) and TriMet's "Coho Commute" sculpture (2019).

## Tualatin business of the week

A morning of art-spotting pairs nicely with a hearty breakfast. A favorite is **Miller's Homestead**, a fixture known for its rustic decor, all-day breakfast menu and homestyle cooking. You'll find it at 17933 SW McEwan Rd, Tualatin, OR 97224.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is the Lazy River sculpture in Tualatin?**
At Tualatin Commons Park, at the intersection of SW Martinazzi Ave, SW Nyberg St and SW Boones Ferry Rd.

**What does the Lazy River sculpture represent?**
Its shape represents the Tualatin River and a mastodon tusk, and it's covered with 32 embossed icons of Tualatin's cultural and natural history. It was installed in August 2014 for the city's centennial.

**How much public art does Tualatin have?**
The City of Tualatin says there are over 250 pieces of public art throughout the city, including paintings, statues and signal box wraps.

**When is the deadline for the signal box wrap call to artists?**
December 2, 2026. Selected artists receive a $500 honorarium per box.
